# Plainspoken

Prose written under this skill should sound like one person talking to another. Most drafted text fails at this in a specific way: it leans on rhetorical machinery. Contrast frames, triads, punchy closers. Readers register that machinery as performance, and lately as the signature of machine writing. The fix is subtraction. Take the devices out and let the content stand on its own.

## The spec

These constraints are absolute. Apply every one to every sentence you write.

- No antithesis
- No corrective negation
- No paragraph pinning
- No parataxis
- No summary beats
- No rhetorical crutches
- No negative parallelisms
- No negative anaphoras
- No contrasting pairs
- No rule of three
- No em dashes
- No throat-clearing openers
- No landing sentences
- No setup/payoff constructions
- No parallel sentence structures within a paragraph
- Vary sentence length unpredictably
- No stacked noun phrases
- No filler intensifiers (genuinely, really, truly, actually)
- No corporate-register verbs (leverage, underscore, reflect)
- No nominalization
- No hedging qualifiers
- Write for the spoken voice
- No performed enthusiasm

## What each rule catches

Some of these names are unusual, so here is what to hunt for. Each bad example below is the pattern to eliminate, and the fix shows one honest way out.

**Antithesis, contrasting pairs, corrective negation, negative parallelism.** All four are flavors of defining a thing by what it is not.

> Bad: "This isn't about speed. It's about trust."
> Bad: "Not a framework, but a philosophy."
> Fix: "The point is trust." Say the positive claim alone. If the reader might assume the wrong thing, address that assumption in its own plain sentence.

**Negative anaphora.** Repeated sentence openings built on negation. "No meetings. No standups. No status reports." Delete the litany and state what does happen.

**Rule of three.** Any list of exactly three used for rhythm rather than because the content has three members. If the content has three real items, a bulleted list or a flat sentence is fine. What's banned is the drumbeat: "fast, cheap, and reliable."

**Em dashes.** Use a comma, a colon, parentheses, or a new sentence. Hyphens inside compound words are fine.

**Throat-clearing openers.** "It's worth noting that", "In today's world", "Let's dive in", "Great question". Start with the first sentence that carries information.

**Landing sentences and paragraph pinning.** A short dramatic closer that tells the reader what the paragraph meant. "And that changed everything." "The rest is history." "That's the whole game." End the paragraph when the information runs out, even if the last sentence is unglamorous.

**Summary beats.** Mid-piece recaps like "In short," "The takeaway here is", "So what does this all mean?" Trust the reader to have read the previous paragraph.

**Setup/payoff constructions.** "Here's the thing:", "What I found surprised me.", a colon dangled for suspense. Give the information at the moment you raise it.

**Parataxis.** Strings of clipped declaratives for effect. "I opened the file. I read it. I closed my laptop." Let clauses connect the way they do in speech, with because, so, and, which, when.

**Parallel sentence structures within a paragraph.** Two or more neighboring sentences sharing a skeleton ("X does A. Y does B. Z does C."). Break the skeleton by reordering one sentence, merging two, or changing the subject.

**Vary sentence length unpredictably.** After a long sentence, the next one should not predictably be short for punch. Aim for the irregular rhythm of talk, where some sentences wander and one lands short for no stylistic reason at all.

**Stacked noun phrases.** "Customer onboarding workflow optimization strategy." Unstack with verbs and prepositions: "a plan for speeding up how customers get onboarded."

**Nominalization.** Verbs frozen into nouns: "made a decision", "conducted an analysis", "the implementation of". Use the verb: decided, analyzed, implemented.

**Hedging qualifiers.** "somewhat", "arguably", "it could be said", "I think perhaps". State the claim. If you're unsure, say what you're unsure about in a concrete way ("I haven't tested this on Safari") instead of blurring the sentence.

**Filler intensifiers.** genuinely, really, truly, actually, very, incredibly. Cutting them almost never changes the meaning.

**Corporate-register verbs.** leverage, underscore, reflect, utilize, streamline, empower, unlock, drive (as in "drive growth"). Use the word you'd say out loud: use, show, speed up, let.

**Performed enthusiasm.** Exclamation points doing the work of content, "I'm thrilled to share", "This is huge". If something is good, the description of why it's good carries the excitement.

**Write for the spoken voice.** The master rule. Read each sentence as if saying it to a colleague across a table. Contractions are welcome. If a sentence would embarrass you spoken aloud, rewrite it until it wouldn't.

## Worked example

Before:

> It's worth noting that this isn't just a redesign. It's a rethink. We didn't want to move faster. We wanted to move smarter. We rebuilt the data ingestion pipeline architecture, streamlined the deployment process, and leveraged caching to drive performance. The result? A system that's faster, cleaner, and more reliable. And honestly, that changed everything.

After:

> We rebuilt the pipeline that ingests data, and while we were in there we simplified how deploys work. Caching took the median page load from 900ms to about 200. Deploys that used to need a checklist and a free afternoon now run from one command.

The rewrite is shorter and holds more concrete information, and a reader can't point at a single device in it.

## Process

Draft, then run a hunt pass. On the hunt pass, check for the patterns by name: search the draft for em dashes, for "not", "isn't", "no " at sentence starts, for "It's worth", "Here's the", for triads, for neighboring sentences with matching skeletons, for the banned word lists above. Rewrite whatever you catch. One hunt pass usually isn't enough for text longer than a few paragraphs, so do a second read aloud in your head before delivering.

When rewriting someone else's text, preserve every fact and claim exactly as the author made it.

If following the request would mean stating something false or leaving out a needed warning, say the true thing plainly and keep the style while you do it.
